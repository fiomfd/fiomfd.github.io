Teaching Policy

I believe that calculus and linear algebra provide fundamental skills for many students. Recently, many English‑speaking universities have introduced “Computational Thinking” courses, which clearly demonstrate the power of classical mathematics for solving and visualizing problems. To help students benefit from this trend, I reduce concepts, theories, and examples to the level of the four arithmetic operations and comparisons of real numbers, and I provide slow, step‑by‑step explanations. This approach is not always popular—it demands accurate understanding and honest work—but I balance it with a realistic assessment policy: students who can perform basic arithmetic correctly on exams will receive credit.

This approach is simply a reflection of how I learned mathematics myself. When I studied topics such as calculus, linear algebra, general topology, real and complex analysis, manifolds, algebra, and functional analysis, I never read a proof or a solution before trying to construct it on my own—even when I already knew the statement was correct. This habit served two purposes. First, training creativity: a mathematician must be able to discover mathematical structures, and practicing with known correct statements is how one develops that ability. Second, preparing to teach: I knew that if I ever became a mathematician, I would be responsible for delivering solid, accurate lectures—a level of understanding far beyond what is needed to pass an exam.

Modernizing and Visualizing Mathematics Courses

In 2022, I learned the course MIT 18.S191 entitled “Introduction to Computational Thinking” on YouTube. I was captivated by the diverse mathematical topics dynamically implemented within the interactive notebook environment. On the other hand, recent webinars on applied mathematics sometimes cover topics that are roughly understandable even to those with only a basic understanding of calculus and linear algebra. Therefore, I’ve come to believe that adding some elementary topics in applied mathematics to my lectures would be beneficial for my students’ future studies and career development. Inspired by these experiences, I have been working on modernizing and visualizing my mathematics courses. More precisely, I base my lectures on world-standard content, adding new topics within reasonable limits, and I use interactive notebooks to conduct demonstrations that students can replicate.

Here are two specific examples. In my multivariable calculus course, I show vertical sections of the graph of a two‑variable function together with its tangent plane. This visualization helps students understand the relationship between total differentiability and partial derivatives, as well as the geometric meaning of the chain rule. In linear algebra, I explain the singular value decomposition and best low‑rank approximation, then demonstrate how an RGB image can be approximated by manipulating the rank.

In 2024, I was in charge of a practice teaching seminar where the course design was left to the instructor. I asked students to create a visualization or experimental work in mathematics using GeoGebra or Python. Although our department does not offer programming or modelling courses, and most students were initially unfamiliar with the software, almost all of them produced excellent work by the final presentation.
